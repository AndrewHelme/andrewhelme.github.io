What happened to your tax bill

If your tax bill made your stomach drop, you’re not alone — and you’re not wrong. Here is the whole story, start to finish, in the order it happened.

Why did my New Castle County tax bill go up?

Two separate things happened, one after the other. It helps to keep them apart.

First, the reassessment. New Castle County had not done a general property reassessment in more than forty years. A court ruling required one. The county hired an outside firm to put a current value on every property, and new values went out to owners in late 2024.

Second, the tax increase. That’s separate. In May 2026, county council voted to raise the county property tax rate by 17%.

Most households felt both, back to back.

Why did homes go up while big commercial properties went down?

Because home values had risen far more since the 1980s than the county’s books ever reflected. When everything was finally re-priced at once, the shift was dramatic. By the county’s own numbers, residential assessments rose about 477% on average, while non-residential rose about 233%.

The practical effect: homeowners went from carrying roughly two-thirds of the county’s property tax burden to roughly three-quarters of it. Some well-known commercial properties — including Amazon’s Boxwood facility and JPMorgan’s Wilmington headquarters — saw their assessments go down.

Reassessment itself is not supposed to be a tax increase. It’s supposed to redistribute the same burden more fairly. But when the redistribution moves that much weight onto homeowners in a single year, “revenue neutral” on a spreadsheet is a very different experience at the kitchen table.

What was the emergency special session about?

The first bills went out in July 2025 and the reaction was immediate. In August 2025 the General Assembly held a special session and passed a law letting New Castle County school districts charge different rates for residential and non-residential property, to soften the hit on homeowners.

School districts then had to redo rates they had already mailed. Brandywine School District cut its residential rate by 15.8% from bills that were already in mailboxes. A group of commercial property owners sued; the courts upheld the law. The payment deadline had to be pushed to December 31, 2025, because the rollout was that chaotic.

At legislative hearings, Kent and Sussex counties were praised for how they communicated with residents. New Castle County was criticized for a lack of transparency.

What is the 17% county tax increase?

In March 2026, the county executive announced a $42 million structural deficit — the result of years of covering permanent, every-year costs with one-time federal pandemic money. When that money ran out, the costs stayed.

His proposed fix was a 17.2% county property tax increase. On May 26, 2026, council passed it — the first county property tax increase in eight years. For a median home, that’s roughly $102 more per year, on top of whatever the reassessment did.

Two council members voted no. One of them, a Democrat, said the deficit came from “kicking the can down the road,” and that the vote told residents they had to foot the bill for a government that had been incompetent. His words: “Eventually this charade has to stop.”

Who actually controls what

This part matters, and most campaign literature won’t tell you honestly. Your total property tax bill has several pieces, and the county controls only one of them.

  • County property tax rate and county budget — controlled by county council. This is the job on the ballot.
  • School taxes — the largest share of most bills. Set by school boards and the state, not by county council.
  • Your assessed value — produced by the county’s reassessment process, which council is responsible for overseeing.
  • Roads — almost all Delaware roads are maintained by the state, not the county.

Any candidate who promises to fix all of it is either confused or counting on you being confused. What a councilman can genuinely do is control the county’s own rate, demand real oversight of county spending, and insist the next reassessment is handled competently.

How do I appeal my assessment?

Appeals are handled through New Castle County’s Board of Assessment Review, and there are firm filing deadlines each year. Start at the county’s assessment office for current deadlines, forms, and the property record for your parcel — and check that record for factual errors first, like wrong square footage, wrong lot size, or a finished basement you don’t have.

We’ll keep current deadlines and links posted here as the county publishes them. If you need help finding the right office, get in touch — that’s constituent service, and it shouldn’t require an election.

What happens next — and what I’d do

By law, reassessment now happens every five years. The next one lands around 2030. The question isn’t whether it happens; it’s whether anyone is watching this time.

In 2022 I ran for this same seat and said the county needed an independent group to analyze the budget and forecast the county’s financial health — specifically to prevent future property and sewer tax increases. That was before the reassessment bills, before the special session, before the lawsuits, before the 17%. I lost, and none of it was done.

So here’s the plan, and it comes in a specific order:

  • Audit the waste. A full public performance audit of county government.
  • Collect what’s already owed. Unpaid fees, fines, and licensing obligations.
  • Make public assets earn. Grants, leases, and land that lowers its own upkeep costs.
  • Grow the tax base, not the tax rate. More taxpayers, not higher taxes on the ones already here.

All four come before anyone reaches into your pocket again.
