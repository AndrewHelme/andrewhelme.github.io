What happened to your New Castle County tax bill

If your tax bill made your stomach drop, you’re not alone and you’re not wrong.

Why did my New Castle County tax bill go up?

Two separate things happened, one after the other. It helps to keep them apart.

First, the reassessment. New Castle County had not done a general property reassessment in more than forty years. A 2021 Court of Chancery settlement required one. The county hired Tyler Technologies to put a current value on every property, and tentative values went out in November 2024. Every property is now assessed at 100 percent of its fair market value as of July 1, 2024.

Second, the tax increase. That’s separate, and it came later. On May 26, 2026, county council raised the county property tax rate by 17.2 percent and sewer consumption rates by 5 percent.

Most households felt both, back to back.

Why did homes go up while big commercial properties went down?

Because home values had risen far more since 1983 than the county’s books ever reflected. When every property was finally re-priced at once, the shift was dramatic. County Executive Marcus Henry told a special legislative committee that average residential assessments rose 477 percent, while average non-residential assessments rose 233 percent.

The practical effect: homeowners went from carrying roughly two thirds of the property tax burden to roughly three quarters of it. Several well known commercial properties came in far below what critics argued they were actually worth.

One point in fairness to the county. A reassessment is not supposed to be a tax increase, and for 2025 the county did adopt what it called a revenue neutral strategy. It did not collect more property tax revenue in 2025 than in 2024. The problem was never that the county collected more. It’s that the same total got redistributed onto homeowners in a single year, and nobody at the county saw it coming or warned anybody.

Did the commercial assessments ever get fixed?

Partly. Slowly. And only after a year of people complaining.

The example that made people angriest was a parking garage. JPMorgan Chase bought the lot next to its Wilmington headquarters for $7 million in 2015, then spent an estimated $15 million putting up a seven story garage on it. The county’s vendor assessed the finished property at $410,000, roughly what a modest Wilmington area house is worth. A state legislator representing an area hit hard by residential increases singled it out publicly and called for more transparency about how the assessments were reached.

It wasn’t one building. A Spotlight Delaware analysis of more than 50 of the county’s largest commercial properties, worth a combined $3.2 billion in assessed value, found the county and its school districts would take in about $6 million less from them than the year before.

Then, in July 2026, the county went back and reviewed the three most contested properties: the Amazon warehouse near Newport, a newly built Buccini Pollin apartment complex, and that parking garage. All three rose by hundreds of millions of dollars in assessed value. The Amazon assessment tripled.

Let me be fair about what that means, because I don’t think you’re served by a campaign that only tells you the bad half. Correcting those numbers was the right outcome, and the county did it. What I’d ask is why it took a year, a special legislative session, two court decisions, and sustained public pressure to get a second look at three buildings, when a homeowner who was overassessed got one appeal window that closed in March and no second look at all.

And temper your expectations on relief. The Christina School District’s chief financial officer told Spotlight Delaware that the data at that point showed roughly $600 million more in non-residential value against roughly $400 million less in residential value. That’s a net gain of about $200 million, and set against legislation lowering commercial rates, he cautioned that residential bills may not come down much. He described it as a convoluted calculation rather than a straight line, and he’s right.

That’s the whole argument for oversight in one story. The numbers were wrong. Residents caught it, not the county. It took a year to fix three of them. Nobody has checked the rest.

What was the emergency special session about?

The first bills went out in July 2025 and the reaction was immediate. On August 12, 2025 the General Assembly held a special session and passed House Bill 242, letting New Castle County school districts charge different rates for residential and non-residential property.

School districts then had to redo rates they had already mailed. All five adopted split rates. A group of apartment and mobile home operators sued to overturn the law. The Court of Chancery upheld it in October 2025 and the Delaware Supreme Court affirmed in November. The county had to reissue bills, and Senate Bill 206 pushed the payment deadline all the way to December 31, 2025.

At legislative hearings, Kent and Sussex counties were praised for how they communicated with residents. New Castle County was criticized for a lack of transparency.

What is the 17 percent county tax increase?

In his March 2026 budget address, the county executive announced a $42 million structural deficit. It came from years of covering permanent, every year costs with one time federal pandemic money. When that money ran out, the costs stayed. In his own words, the deficit turned out to be “much, much larger than what I was shown.”

His fix was a 17.2 percent property tax increase, raising about $23 million toward the $42 million gap. On May 26, 2026, council passed it 11 to 2. It was the first county property tax increase since 2019. For the median county home, assessed around $378,100, that is about $102 more per year, roughly $8.50 a month, on top of whatever the reassessment did.

To close the rest of the gap, the county also cut about 10 percent from library budgets, $2.5 million from open space and Agricultural Preservation, and $2.8 million from Community Services, and left 56 positions unfunded.

One of the two members who voted no, a Democrat, said the deficit came from “kicking the can down the road,” and that the vote told residents they had to foot the bill for a government that had been incompetent. His words: “Eventually this charade has to stop.”

And the sewer increase?

The same budget raised sewer consumption rates by 5 percent, effective July 1, 2026. The county puts the average impact at about $13.70 a year. Like the property tax, it was the first sewer rate increase since 2019.

On its own that is a small number, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise. It matters because of the timing. Property taxes and sewer rates went up in the same vote, on the same night, on top of a reassessment year. For a household on a fixed income in Brandywine Hundred, those don’t arrive as separate line items. They arrive as one bill that is bigger than last year’s.

When will my 2026 tax bill arrive?

Later than usual, and later than the election.

The General Assembly passed Senate Bill 228, which lets New Castle County run a quality control review of non-residential assessments. The county hired Cushman & Wakefield to review a random sample of 50 non-residential properties, with that work expected to wrap up in early September 2026.

The assessment roll can’t be finalized until the review is done. So the county now expects to mail tax bills in mid-November 2026, with payment due December 31, 2026. If the review finds that a property owner’s effective tax obligation should go up, that owner keeps the right to challenge the decision under Delaware law.

This is the second year running that New Castle County tax bills have landed late and come due on New Year’s Eve. Checking the numbers is the right thing to do. Doing it a year after the bills went out, twice, is the part that ought to bother you. Getting it right the first time is what oversight is for.

Who actually controls what

This part matters, and most campaign literature won’t tell you straight. Your total property tax bill has several pieces and the county controls only one of them.

  • County property tax rate, sewer rates, and the county budget. Set by county council. That is the job on this ballot.
  • School taxes. The largest share of most bills, and not the county’s decision. Rates are set by school boards. The county’s own bill explainer tells residents to contact their school district or NCC Vo-Tech with questions, because “New Castle County is only the billing administrator for school taxes.”
  • Your assessed value. Produced by the county’s reassessment process and its vendor, which county council is responsible for overseeing. That is where the oversight failed.
  • Roads. DelDOT maintains 89 percent of Delaware’s roadways. We’re one of only about three states where the state maintains essentially the entire public road network. Your county councilman doesn’t pave your street.

Any candidate who promises to fix all of it is either confused or counting on you being confused. What a councilman can actually do is vote on the county’s own rates and budget, demand real oversight of county spending and county vendors, insist the next reassessment is handled competently, and help you get an answer when the county owes you one.

How do I appeal my assessment?

Fix a factual error. Any time, no deadline.

This is the part most people miss. If the county’s record of your property is simply wrong, wrong square footage, wrong number of bedrooms, wrong condition, a finished basement you don’t have, New Castle County says you can ask for a correction by contacting the Assessment Office directly, whether or not you filed an appeal on time.

  • Email: assessment@newcastlede.gov
  • Phone: (302) 395-5520
  • Check your record first: New Castle County parcel search. Pull up your parcel and read what the county thinks it owns.

Start there before anything else. A factual error is the easiest thing to get fixed and it doesn’t require a hearing.

File a formal appeal. January 1 to March 14.

Formal appeals go to the Board of Assessment Review, which is independent of the Assessment Division. The Board can lower an assessment. By rule it cannot raise one. There is no cost to appeal.

  • Deadline. March 14 each year, received by 4:00 p.m. A postmark doesn’t count. It has to actually get there.
  • Where to send it. Email BOARAppeals@newcastlede.gov, or deliver by hand or mail to the Office of Finance, Assessment Division, New Castle County Government Center, 87 Reads Way, New Castle, DE 19720.
  • Forms. Residential Appeal Application (PDF) and Non-Residential Appeal Application (PDF).
  • Bring your evidence up front. Only evidence submitted with your original appeal form can be discussed at the hearing. Comparable sales, photographs, repair estimates, an appraisal. Assemble it before you file, not after.
  • Know the standard. Your assessment is meant to reflect 100 percent of fair market value as of July 1, 2024. That’s the number you’re arguing about. Not what your house is worth today, and not what your tax bill says.
  • One form per parcel, filled out completely. An incomplete form can be denied as deficient without a hearing on the merits.

If the Board rules against you, its decision can be appealed to Delaware Superior Court within 30 days.

If you’re 65 or older

Delaware offers a Senior School Property Tax Credit, and House Bill 159 expanded school tax relief. The county executive reported that more than 7,000 New Castle County seniors qualified. Eligibility and application deadlines are separate from the appeal process. Check the county’s exemptions page or call Assessment at (302) 395-5520.

Official pages. Go straight to the source.

  • Board of Assessment Review. Who they are and how hearings work.
  • Assessment Division. Forms, exemptions, contacts.
  • Rules of Procedure. The actual rules governing appeals.
  • Tax Information and Forms. Rates, billing dates, penalties.

Deadlines and forms are set by the county and they change. Always confirm on the county’s own pages before you file. Official information beats a campaign website, including this one. And if you’re stuck finding the right office, get in touch. That’s constituent service, and it shouldn’t require an election.

What happens next, and what I’d do

By law, reassessment now comes around every five years. The next one lands about 2030. The question isn’t whether it happens. It’s whether anyone is watching this time.

In 2022 I ran for this same seat and said the county needed an independent group to analyze the budget and forecast our financial health, specifically to prevent future property and sewer tax increases. That was before the reassessment bills, before the special session, before the lawsuits, before the 17 percent and the 5 percent. I lost, and none of it got done. Here’s what I actually said, and why I’m running again.

So here’s the plan, and the order matters:

  • Audit the waste. A full public performance audit of county government.
  • Collect what’s already owed. Unpaid fees, fines, and licensing obligations.
  • Make public assets earn. Grants, leases, and land that costs less to keep.
  • Grow the tax base, not the tax rate. More taxpayers, not higher taxes on the ones already here.

All four come before anyone reaches into your pocket again.
