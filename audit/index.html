The plan

Audit first. Taxes last.

Families balance budgets. Businesses justify spending. County government should meet the same standard — and right now it doesn’t have to.

The performance audit

My first act on council: propose the first comprehensive, independent, public performance audit of county government in years. Not a compliance check on whether the paperwork was filed correctly. A review of whether taxpayers are getting value.

Every department answers one question: are residents getting the best possible value for every dollar spent?

The audit examines:

  • Vendor contracts and consulting agreements
  • Procurement practices and competitive bidding
  • Overtime spending and fleet operations
  • Software subscriptions and recurring services
  • Long-vacant budgeted positions
  • Administrative processes that technology could modernize

And the findings get published where residents can read them — not summarized in a meeting nobody attends.

Why this matters now

County budgets grow because programs roll over year after year without anyone asking whether they still work. That’s convenient. It isn’t responsible stewardship.

Here is what that habit produced: permanent costs covered with one-time federal money, a $42 million hole when the money ran out, a reassessment rollout the legislature had to clean up in a special session — and then a 17% tax increase, presented as though it were the only option available.

It wasn’t the only option. It was the easiest one.

The order of operations

Four things that come before your wallet

Raising the rate is the last resort, not the first move. Here is the order I’d work in.

Audit out the waste

The performance audit above. Savings get reinvested in what residents actually care about: public safety, parks, infrastructure, and community services.

Collect what’s already owed

Unpaid fees, fines, and licensing obligations are money the county has already earned and simply hasn’t collected. Chasing it is less painful than raising rates on people who pay on time.

Make public assets earn

The county owns thousands of acres. Some of it costs money every year and returns almost nothing but another round of mowing. Convert selected roadsides and stormwater basins to native meadows: fewer mowings, lower maintenance costs, better stormwater absorption, and habitat for monarchs and honeybees. Pursue conservation and stormwater grants that bring outside money in. Lease sites for EV charging rather than owning and operating them.

Grow the tax base, not the tax rate

Permitting that’s fast and predictable for homeowners and small businesses. Vacant properties returned to productive use. More taxpayers sharing the load, instead of a heavier load on the taxpayers already here.

We don’t need to raise taxes. We need to raise taxpayers.

Three questions, every time

Before county government spends a dollar that belongs to somebody else, I ask:

  • Will it work?
  • Do we need it?
  • Can we afford it?

If any answer is no, we think twice. That’s not a political philosophy. It’s how your household already runs.

“Government should be audited like every business. Eliminate waste before asking taxpayers to pay more.” Jamie Collins
